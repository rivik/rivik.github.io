THE HONEST READ

A calm average, hiding brutal spikes

The average looks calm

Most minutes the path is only a few percent slower than usual — that's why casual browsing feels fine. We show that honestly, model and all; the headline number is deliberately un-scary.

The spikes are brutal

During a documented jam the same path runs 45–90% worse — effectively timeouts. One is happening right now on US-West, and over a month they keep coming.

Your path decides your fate

Same country — even the same host — can ride 9% or 69% of its traffic over one carrier. Low-diversity paths feel every Cogent jam; a proxy doing thousands of requests feels every spike.

The one thing that actually works

You can't move the roads. You can move your source.

You can't pick your route across the ocean, and you can't move the US services you talk to — nobody can. But you can move your own source onto the US side: run the server there, right next to those services. That takes the Atlantic — and every jam on it — out of the path.

Method. The number is the time-average, over 30 days (2 Jun – 2 Jul, frozen 12:00 UTC), of a modeled “% worse than usual” curve: a labeled baseline (typical evening congestion on shared links — not measured on your circuit) with a spike for each publicly documented incident (real dates & durations; peak heights modeled). The Jul 2 Cogent spike is the exception — drawn from our own measurement (0.5–0.9% packet loss, rising; height derived from it). Path diversity is measured for Germany (RIPE RIS: identical hosts split 9% Frankfurt vs 69% Zurich onto Cogent AS174) and modeled from market topology for other countries. A US or transit fault sits on the shared road, so it counts for every source reaching that coast, weighted by how much that source depends on the affected carrier. Documented events only — a floor; real disruption is higher. Sources: NetworkWorld outage log, The National (22 Jun fiber cut), ThousandEyes, Cloudflare Radar; Cogent AS174 US-West from our own mtr / RIPE RIS, 2 Jul 2026. Coastlines: Natural Earth. Cables drawn between real landing points.
